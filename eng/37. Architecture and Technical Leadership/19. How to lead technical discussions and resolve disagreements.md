# How to lead technical discussions and resolve disagreements

---

## 🟢 Junior Level

Technical disagreements are normal and healthy — they surface risks and better options. The goal is a **good decision made in reasonable time**, with people committed to it, not "winning the argument".

### Basics of a productive discussion
1. **Agree on the problem** before debating solutions: what are we optimizing for (latency, delivery speed, cost, simplicity)?
2. **Separate facts from opinions**: measurements, constraints and requirements vs preferences.
3. **List options** (at least two, including "do nothing" or "simplest thing") with pros, cons and risks.
4. **Decide how to decide**: who is the decision-maker, by when, and what would change the decision.
5. **Write it down** (an ADR) so the reasoning isn't lost.

### Phrases that help
* "What problem are we solving, and how will we know it's solved?"
* "What would have to be true for option B to be the better choice?"
* "Can we test this cheaply before committing?" (spike, prototype, benchmark)
* "Disagree and commit: I prefer A, but I'll support B fully."

---

## 🟡 Middle Level

### Structuring a technical decision
A lightweight RFC/design document:
* **Context** — current state, constraints, requirements (functional and non-functional, with numbers).
* **Options** — each with description, trade-offs, cost, risk, reversibility.
* **Recommendation** — and why.
* **Open questions** — what we don't know yet.
* **Decision** — recorded as an ADR with date, deciders and status.

### Using evidence to resolve disputes
* **Benchmarks** with realistic workloads (JMH for code paths, load tests for services) instead of intuition about performance.
* **Prototypes/spikes** time-boxed to 1–3 days to retire the biggest uncertainty.
* **Data** from production: traces, error rates, incidents, cost reports.
* **Reversibility test** (Bezos' "one-way vs two-way doors"): if a decision is cheap to reverse, decide fast and learn; if irreversible (data model, public API, vendor lock-in), invest more in analysis.

### Facilitating meetings
* Share the doc before the meeting; use the meeting for questions and decision, not for reading.
* Invite the people affected and those with relevant expertise — not everyone.
* Timebox; make sure quiet people speak (ask directly); summarize agreements and action items at the end.

## 🔴 Senior Level

### When consensus doesn't happen
* Clarify **decision rights** upfront (RACI, team ownership, architecture guild for cross-team standards). The owning team decides for its service; cross-cutting decisions go to a designated forum or lead.
* Escalate **early and neutrally**: present both options fairly to the decision-maker; escalation is a process, not a failure.
* **Disagree and commit** — once decided, everyone executes wholeheartedly; revisit only with new evidence, at an agreed checkpoint ("we'll review after the load test in 3 weeks").

### Common dysfunctions and fixes
* **Bikeshedding** (endless debate on trivial topics) → timebox, defer to existing conventions, let the owner decide.
* **HiPPO** (highest-paid person's opinion wins) → ask for criteria and evidence; leaders should speak last.
* **Analysis paralysis** → identify reversibility; run a spike; set a decision date.
* **Resurfacing decisions** → ADRs with rationale; require new information to reopen.
* **Personal conflict disguised as technical** → move to a 1:1 conversation; focus on interests (what each person worries about) rather than positions.

### Influence without authority
As a senior/staff engineer you often lead without managing:
* Build **trust** by delivering, listening and giving credit.
* **Write well** — clear docs scale your influence beyond meetings.
* Understand **stakeholders' incentives** (product wants speed, ops wants stability, security wants risk reduction) and frame proposals in their terms.
* **Make the right thing easy**: templates, tooling, paved roads beat mandates.

### Interview example (STAR)
"Two teams disagreed on Kafka vs synchronous REST for order-to-billing integration. I wrote a short RFC framing the requirements — billing could lag by minutes, but checkout availability couldn't depend on billing. We ran a two-day spike measuring failure scenarios. The data showed REST coupling dropped checkout availability during billing deploys; we chose Kafka with an outbox. The REST advocate's concern about debuggability became action items: tracing through Kafka headers and a replay tool. Both teams committed, and the ADR prevented the debate from resurfacing six months later."

### A one-page decision record template

```markdown
# ADR-017: Integrate order and billing via Kafka events

Status: Accepted (2026-10-16) · Deciders: checkout lead, billing lead, architect

## Context
Billing may lag by minutes; checkout availability must not depend on billing deploys.

## Options
1. Synchronous REST call from checkout — simple; couples availability (measured: 0.4% checkout errors during billing deploys).
2. Kafka event via transactional outbox — decoupled; needs tracing and replay tooling.

## Decision
Option 2. Revisit if end-to-end lag p99 exceeds 60 s for two consecutive weeks.

## Consequences
+ Checkout independent of billing availability.
- New tooling: trace propagation through Kafka headers, DLQ dashboard, replay runbook (owner: billing).
```

## 🎯 Interview Cheat Sheet

**30-second pitch:** I lead technical discussions by agreeing on the problem and decision criteria first, laying out options with trade-offs and reversibility, using evidence — benchmarks, spikes, production data — over opinions, clarifying who decides, and recording the outcome in an ADR. When consensus fails I escalate neutrally and practice disagree-and-commit with a checkpoint to revisit, and I avoid bikeshedding, HiPPO and analysis paralysis.

**Must know:**
* Problem → criteria → options → decision process → ADR.
* RFC structure; evidence types; one-way vs two-way doors.
* Meeting facilitation basics.
* Decision rights, escalation, disagree and commit.
* Dysfunctions and fixes; influence without authority.

**Common follow-ups:**
* Tell me about a technical disagreement you resolved.
* What if your manager overrules a decision you think is wrong?
* How do you stop old debates from coming back?

**Red flags:** winning by seniority; endless meetings without decisions; refusing to commit after a decision.
