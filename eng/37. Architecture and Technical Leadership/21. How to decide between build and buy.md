# How to decide between build and buy

---

## 🟢 Junior Level

For any capability — authentication, search, payments, feature flags, workflow engine, observability, CMS — teams choose between:
* **Build** — develop and operate it in-house.
* **Buy** — a SaaS product or commercial software (Auth0/Okta, Stripe, LaunchDarkly, Datadog).
* **Adopt open source** — self-host an existing project (Keycloak, OpenSearch, Unleash, Temporal), or use a **managed** version of it (Amazon OpenSearch, Temporal Cloud).

### The key question
**Is this capability a differentiator for our business?**
* **Core / differentiating** (what customers choose you for: your pricing engine, matching algorithm, unique workflow) → usually **build** and own it.
* **Commodity / supporting** (login, email delivery, payments processing, monitoring) → usually **buy or adopt**; your engineers' time is better spent on the core.

### Hidden costs of building
Building is not just initial development: maintenance, security patches, scaling, on-call, documentation, onboarding, and opportunity cost (what else the team could have built). A rule of thumb: the cost of operating and evolving software over its lifetime is several times the cost of writing it.

## 🟡 Middle Level

### Evaluation criteria

| Criterion | Questions |
| --- | --- |
| **Fit** | Does it meet functional and non-functional requirements (latency, scale, data residency)? |
| **Total cost of ownership (3–5 years)** | Licenses/usage fees vs engineering salaries for build + operations + on-call |
| **Time to value** | Weeks with a vendor vs months to build |
| **Integration** | APIs, SDKs for Java/Spring, webhooks, SSO, data export |
| **Lock-in & exit** | Can we export data? Standard protocols (OIDC, OpenTelemetry, S3 API)? Contract terms? |
| **Security & compliance** | SOC 2/ISO 27001, GDPR/DPA, data location, pen-test reports |
| **Vendor viability** | Financial health, roadmap, support quality, community activity (for OSS) |
| **Customization** | Can it handle our edge cases, or will we fight it? |
| **Operational load** | Who runs it, upgrades it, gets paged? |

### Simple TCO comparison (illustrative)

```text
Feature flags for 40 engineers, 30 services:
  Build: 2 engineers × 3 months initial (~0.5 FTE-year) + 0.3 FTE/year maintenance & on-call
         ≈ 0.5 + 5 × 0.3 = 2.0 FTE-years over 5 years
  Buy:   SaaS ≈ 25 k€/year ≈ 125 k€ over 5 years + 0.05 FTE/year integration upkeep
  Self-host OSS (Unleash): 0.1 FTE-year setup + 0.15 FTE/year ops + infra
Decision: buy or self-host OSS; building only wins if flags are a product differentiator.
```

### Reducing lock-in when buying
* Put the vendor behind your own **interface** (a `FeatureFlags` or `PaymentGateway` port) — or use open standards: **OpenFeature** for flags, **OpenTelemetry** for observability, **OIDC/SAML** for identity, **S3-compatible APIs** for storage.
* Keep **data export** paths tested; avoid vendor-specific features in core flows unless the value is clear.

## 🔴 Senior Level

### Decision process for significant choices
1. **Define requirements and constraints** with stakeholders (including security, legal, finance) — must-haves vs nice-to-haves, scale numbers, compliance.
2. **Shortlist options** (build, 2–3 vendors, 1–2 OSS) with a weighted scoring matrix.
3. **Run a time-boxed proof of concept** on the riskiest requirements (performance at our scale, integration with Spring Security, data residency).
4. **Model TCO and risks** over 3–5 years, including exit costs.
5. **Record the decision in an ADR** with revisit triggers ("re-evaluate if usage cost exceeds X or vendor changes pricing").

### Nuances that experienced engineers raise
* **"Buy" still requires engineering**: integration, configuration as code, monitoring the vendor, handling outages (fallbacks when the auth provider or payment processor is down), and incident processes with the vendor's support.
* **Pricing models scale differently** — per seat, per MAU, per event/GB. A cheap pilot can become the largest line item at scale (observability and logging vendors are classic examples); negotiate and model growth.
* **Open source isn't free**: license changes (Elastic, Redis, HashiCorp moved to source-available licenses), maintenance burden, security response time; managed offerings reduce ops but reintroduce vendor dependency.
* **Hybrid**: buy the platform, build the differentiating layer on top (e.g. use Stripe for payment processing, build your own subscription and pricing logic).
* **Revisit decisions**: what was commodity can become strategic (and vice versa) as the business evolves.

### Red flags in build decisions
* "Not invented here" — building because it's interesting, not because it differentiates.
* Underestimating security-sensitive domains (authentication, cryptography, payments) — these are rarely worth building.
* No plan for the long tail of features users expect from mature products (admin UIs, audit logs, SSO, permissions, exports).

## 🎯 Interview Cheat Sheet

**30-second pitch:** I build what differentiates the business and buy or adopt what's commodity. The decision weighs fit, three-to-five-year total cost of ownership including operations and opportunity cost, time to value, integration, lock-in and exit paths, security and compliance, and vendor or community viability — validated with a proof of concept on the riskiest requirements and recorded in an ADR with revisit triggers. When buying, I reduce lock-in with ports and open standards like OIDC, OpenTelemetry and OpenFeature.

**Must know:**
* Core vs commodity framing.
* Evaluation criteria and TCO components.
* PoC on riskiest requirements; weighted scoring; ADR.
* Lock-in mitigation via interfaces and standards.
* Pricing scaling, OSS license risk, hybrid approaches.

**Common follow-ups:**
* Would you build your own authentication?
* How do you estimate TCO?
* What if the vendor raises prices 3×?

**Red flags:** building auth/crypto in-house without strong reasons; comparing only license cost vs initial dev time; no exit strategy.
