# Common system design mistakes

---

## 🟢 Junior Level

System design interviews are rarely failed because of a missing technology. They're failed because of **process mistakes**:

1. **Jumping to a solution** before clarifying requirements and scale.
2. **No numbers** — designing for "lots of users" without estimating QPS, data size or growth.
3. **Buzzword architecture** — Kafka, Kubernetes, microservices and five databases for a problem that needs one service and PostgreSQL.
4. **Ignoring non-functional requirements** — availability, latency, consistency, security, cost.
5. **Single points of failure** — one database, one cache node, one region, without discussing them.
6. **Not discussing trade-offs** — presenting choices as obviously right instead of comparing alternatives.
7. **Running out of time** on one component (often the database schema) and never finishing the end-to-end flow.

### A better shape for 45 minutes
* 5 min — requirements (functional + non-functional), assumptions.
* 5 min — estimates (QPS, storage, bandwidth).
* 10 min — high-level design, API, data model.
* 15 min — deep dive into 1–2 critical components (chosen with the interviewer).
* 5–10 min — bottlenecks, failure modes, trade-offs, evolution.

## 🟡 Middle Level

### Technical mistakes interviewers look for
* **Synchronous chains everywhere** — a request fanning out through 6 services in series; availability multiplies down and latency adds up. Use async messaging for non-critical side effects; parallelize required calls.
* **Database as a queue** — polling a table without `SKIP LOCKED`, unbounded growth, contention. Use a broker or a proper job queue design.
* **Cache as the source of truth** — no persistence strategy, data lost on eviction or restart.
* **Unbounded everything** — unbounded queues, thread pools, result sets (`SELECT *` without pagination), payload sizes, retries.
* **No idempotency** — retries create duplicate orders/payments.
* **Dual writes** — writing to the database and the broker/search index separately and assuming both succeed.
* **Ignoring hot spots** — celebrity users, flash sales, popular keys; uniform-load assumptions break.
* **Strong consistency everywhere** (or nowhere) — not distinguishing which data needs it (payments, inventory reservation) from what tolerates staleness (feeds, counts).

### Data model mistakes
* Choosing NoSQL "for scale" without access patterns; choosing SQL and then needing 12 joins on the hot path.
* No indexing discussion for the main queries.
* Sharding prematurely — before vertical scaling, read replicas, caching and partitioning are exhausted — or with a shard key that doesn't match access patterns.

## 🔴 Senior Level

### Mistakes that reveal limited production experience
* **No observability story** — how will you know it's broken? SLIs/SLOs, metrics, tracing, alerting.
* **No failure-mode analysis** — what happens when the cache is down, the broker lags, a region fails, a dependency is slow (slow is worse than down)?
* **No backpressure or load shedding** — the design collapses under 3× traffic instead of degrading.
* **Ignoring operations** — deployments (zero-downtime migrations, backward-compatible schema changes), configuration, secrets, on-call burden of each new component.
* **Ignoring cost** — cross-region traffic, 100% trace sampling, oversized clusters, storing raw events forever.
* **Security as an afterthought** — authentication between services, authorization per resource, encryption, PII handling, abuse (rate limiting, bots).
* **Designing the final state only** — not explaining how to evolve from MVP to scale ("start with a modular monolith and PostgreSQL; extract the feed service when write volume exceeds X").

### Communication mistakes
* Not checking in with the interviewer about which part to deep-dive.
* Drawing boxes without data flows (what calls what, sync or async, with what payload).
* Answering "it depends" without saying *on what* and *which way you'd go given the assumptions*.
* Defending a choice when the interviewer introduces a new constraint — strong candidates adapt the design openly.

### Self-review checklist (say it out loud at the end)
1. Do the numbers work (QPS per component vs its capacity)?
2. What are the single points of failure and how are they mitigated?
3. Where is the source of truth for each piece of data? How are derived copies kept in sync?
4. What is retried, and is it idempotent?
5. What are the consistency guarantees per operation?
6. What happens under 10× load or a dependency outage?
7. How is it observed, deployed and secured?

### A capacity sanity check you can do out loud

```text
Requirement: 20M daily active users, 10 feed reads/user/day, 1 post/user/day, peak = 3x average

Reads:  20M x 10 / 86,400 s  ≈ 2,300 req/s average  -> ~7,000 req/s peak
Writes: 20M x 1  / 86,400 s  ≈   230 req/s average  ->   ~700 req/s peak
Storage: 20M posts/day x 1 KB ≈ 20 GB/day ≈ 7.3 TB/year (+ replication x3 ≈ 22 TB)

Single PostgreSQL primary handles ~700 writes/s comfortably  -> no sharding needed yet
7,000 reads/s -> cache feeds (Redis), read replicas for the rest
Conclusion stated: "start with one primary + replicas + cache; shard by user_id when writes exceed ~5-10k/s"
```

## 🎯 Interview Cheat Sheet

**30-second pitch:** The usual failures are process ones — skipping requirements and estimates, buzzword architectures, no trade-offs, poor time management — and technical ones like synchronous chains, dual writes, missing idempotency, unbounded resources, ignored hot spots and blanket consistency choices. Strong answers quantify, state assumptions, analyze failure modes, cover observability, security, cost and evolution, and end with a self-review.

**Must know:**
* 45-minute structure.
* Synchronous chain availability math.
* Dual writes → outbox; retries → idempotency.
* Hot spots, backpressure, load shedding.
* Self-review checklist.

**Common follow-ups:**
* What would break first at 10× traffic?
* Where's your single point of failure?
* How would you build the MVP version?

**Red flags:** "we'll just add more servers"; no mention of failure; using every technology in the hype cycle.
