# SLA, SLO, SLI and observability

---

## 🟢 Junior Level

These three terms (popularized by Google SRE) define how reliability is measured and promised:

* **SLI (Service Level Indicator)** — a **measurement** of user-visible behavior, as a ratio of good events to total events. Example: "proportion of checkout requests that succeed in under 500 ms".
* **SLO (Service Level Objective)** — an internal **target** for an SLI over a window. Example: "99.9% of checkout requests succeed in < 500 ms over 28 days".
* **SLA (Service Level Agreement)** — an external **contract** with consequences (credits, refunds) if missed. Usually looser than the SLO (e.g. SLA 99.5%, SLO 99.9%) so you get warned before you owe money.

### Error budget
`error budget = 1 − SLO`. With a 99.9% SLO over 28 days, you may "spend" 0.1% of requests (or ~40 minutes of full outage) on failures. The budget turns reliability into a decision tool:
* Budget left → ship features, run experiments, take deployment risk.
* Budget exhausted → prioritize reliability work, slow down risky changes.

| Availability | Downtime per 30 days |
| --- | --- |
| 99% | 7.2 hours |
| 99.9% | 43 minutes |
| 99.95% | 21.6 minutes |
| 99.99% | 4.3 minutes |

## 🟡 Middle Level

### Choosing good SLIs
Measure what users experience, as close to them as possible (load balancer or client telemetry, not CPU):
* **Availability** — `good = non-5xx responses` (decide whether 429 and specific 4xx count as bad).
* **Latency** — `good = requests faster than threshold` (use a threshold on a percentile, e.g. 95% < 300 ms, 99% < 1 s).
* **Freshness** (data pipelines) — `good = data updated within 5 minutes`.
* **Correctness** — `good = results matching a reference` (sampled checks).
* **Durability** — for storage systems.

Define SLIs per **critical user journey** (search, add to cart, checkout), not per microservice.

### Implementing with Prometheus/Micrometer

```promql
# Availability SLI over 28 days for the checkout endpoint
sum(rate(http_server_requests_seconds_count{uri="/api/checkout", status!~"5.."}[28d]))
/
sum(rate(http_server_requests_seconds_count{uri="/api/checkout"}[28d]))

# Latency SLI: share of requests under 500 ms (needs histogram buckets)
sum(rate(http_server_requests_seconds_bucket{uri="/api/checkout", le="0.5"}[28d]))
/
sum(rate(http_server_requests_seconds_count{uri="/api/checkout"}[28d]))
```

In Spring Boot, enable histogram buckets with SLO boundaries:

```yaml
management:
  metrics:
    distribution:
      slo:
        http.server.requests: 100ms, 300ms, 500ms, 1s
```

### Observability pillars
* **Metrics** — cheap aggregates for SLIs, dashboards and alerts (Micrometer → Prometheus).
* **Logs** — detailed events, structured JSON with trace IDs.
* **Traces** — request paths across services (OpenTelemetry / Micrometer Tracing).
* (Increasingly) **profiles** — continuous profiling to explain CPU/allocation regressions.

## 🔴 Senior Level

### Alerting on SLOs: burn-rate alerts
Alerting on raw error rate ("> 1% errors for 5 min") is noisy and misses slow burns. Instead alert on how fast the **error budget is burning**. Burn rate = observed error rate / allowed error rate. For a 99.9% SLO (allowed 0.1%), an error rate of 1.44% is a burn rate of 14.4 — at that pace the 30-day budget is gone in about 2 days.

Google's multi-window, multi-burn-rate recommendation:

| Severity | Burn rate | Long window | Short window | Budget consumed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Page | 14.4 | 1 h | 5 min | 2% in 1 h |
| Page | 6 | 6 h | 30 min | 5% in 6 h |
| Ticket | 1 | 3 days | 6 h | 10% in 3 days |

The short window makes alerts reset quickly once the problem stops. Tools like Sloth or Pyrra generate these Prometheus rules from SLO definitions.

### SLO pitfalls
* **Too many SLOs** — teams ignore them; pick 1–3 per journey.
* **100% targets** — impossible and blocks all change; users can't distinguish 99.99% from 100% when their own network is worse.
* **Averages instead of percentiles/thresholds** — averages hide tail latency that real users feel.
* **Measuring at the wrong layer** — server-side metrics miss failures in DNS, CDN, TLS, client networks; complement with synthetic probes and real-user monitoring.
* **Dependency math** — your SLO can't exceed the product of hard dependencies' availability unless you add redundancy/fallbacks.

### Organizational use
* Error budget policies agreed with product: what happens when the budget is spent (feature freeze, postmortem priority).
* SLO reviews monthly: are targets right? Did users complain while SLOs were green (bad SLI)? Were SLOs red without user impact (too strict)?
* Tie SLOs to deployment: canary analysis compares the canary's SLIs to the baseline and rolls back automatically.

## 🎯 Interview Cheat Sheet

**30-second pitch:** An SLI measures user-visible behavior as good/total events, an SLO is the internal target for that SLI over a window, and an SLA is the external contract with penalties, usually looser than the SLO. The error budget, 1 − SLO, balances feature velocity against reliability. I define SLIs per critical user journey, implement them with histogram-based metrics, and alert with multi-window burn-rate alerts rather than raw thresholds.

**Must know:**
* SLI vs SLO vs SLA; nines → downtime table.
* Error budgets and policies.
* Good SLIs: availability, latency thresholds, freshness, correctness.
* Micrometer SLO histogram buckets; PromQL ratio.
* Burn-rate alerts (14.4× / 6× / 1×).

**Common follow-ups:**
* What SLO would you set for a new service?
* Why not alert on CPU usage?
* How do dependencies affect your achievable SLO?

**Red flags:** 100% SLOs; SLOs on internal metrics nobody feels; alerting on every 5xx spike.
