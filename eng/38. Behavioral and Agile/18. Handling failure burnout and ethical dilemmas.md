# Handling failure burnout and ethical dilemmas

---

## 🟢 Junior Level

These questions test **maturity and self-awareness**: how you handle setbacks, protect your and your team's sustainability, and act with integrity under pressure.

### Failure
Interviewers ask "Tell me about a time you failed" to see whether you:
* **Own** the failure (no blaming others or circumstances).
* **Reflect** on what you'd do differently.
* **Changed something** as a result (behavior, process, system).

Choose a **real** failure with meaningful stakes — not a disguised strength ("I work too hard"). Spend more time on the learning than the failure itself.

### Burnout
Burnout is chronic work stress: exhaustion, cynicism, reduced effectiveness. Healthy answers show you:
* Recognize warning signs (in yourself and teammates).
* Set sustainable boundaries and raise workload issues early.
* Address root causes (on-call load, unclear priorities, constant urgency) rather than heroics.

### Ethical dilemmas
Examples: being asked to ship known security flaws, collect data without consent, hide a data breach, misreport metrics, use unlicensed code. The expected answer: **raise concerns clearly through the right channels, propose alternatives, and refuse to participate in clearly illegal or harmful actions.**

## 🟡 Middle Level

### STAR example: failure

**Situation**: I led the migration of our search from database `LIKE` queries to Elasticsearch, promising product a 4-week timeline.

**Task**: Deliver faster, typo-tolerant search for the catalog.

**Action / what went wrong**: I focused on indexing and query relevance but underestimated keeping the index in sync with catalog changes. We used dual writes from the application; under failures, some products disappeared from search. We launched in week 6 with inconsistencies that support had to handle manually for two weeks.

**Result / learning**: I owned the miss in the retro and with product. We replaced dual writes with Debezium CDC from PostgreSQL into the indexer and added a nightly reconciliation job comparing counts and checksums. Since then I identify **data consistency and failure modes first** in any design involving a second data store, include them explicitly in estimates, and prototype the riskiest part in week one. Later projects with similar shapes (the recommendations store) shipped on time.

### Sustainable work and burnout
* **Make load visible**: track on-call pages, after-hours work, unplanned work; bring data to your manager.
* **Reduce toil**: automate repetitive operational tasks; fix noisy alerts; improve runbooks.
* **Protect focus**: limit work in progress, batch meetings, agree on response-time expectations.
* **Ask for help early** and help others do the same; normalize taking time off.
* As a lead: watch for signs in others (irritability, disengagement, working late constantly), rotate on-call fairly, push back on unrealistic timelines with options.

## 🔴 Senior Level

### STAR example: ethical dilemma

**Situation**: Before a major release, a stakeholder asked us to enable tracking of users' exact location in the mobile app for "future analytics", without updating the privacy notice or asking for consent, because the legal review would delay the launch.

**Task**: Ship the release while protecting users and the company.

**Action**: I explained the risk in business terms (GDPR consent requirements, app store policy violations, potential fines and reputational damage), proposed an alternative (collect only coarse, consented region data and add precise location in a follow-up release after legal review), and escalated through my manager to the privacy/legal team with a written summary. I made it clear I wouldn't implement collection without consent.

**Result**: Legal confirmed consent was required; we shipped with coarse region data behind a consent prompt and added precise location two sprints later with a proper privacy update. The stakeholder later thanked us for avoiding a likely app store rejection.

### Principles to convey
* **Integrity is non-negotiable**; escalate with facts, not accusations.
* **Know the channels**: manager, security/privacy/legal teams, ethics hotlines; document concerns in writing.
* **Offer solutions**, not just objections — most dilemmas have an alternative that meets the legitimate goal.
* **Professional codes** (ACM Code of Ethics) emphasize avoiding harm, honesty and respecting privacy.

### Talking about failure and burnout as a leader
* Share your own failures openly to build psychological safety.
* Treat team burnout as a system problem: chronic overtime is a planning failure; heroics hide problems that should be fixed.
* Measure and act: on-call health metrics, survey results, attrition signals; adjust scope and process.

### Raising an ethical concern in writing

```markdown
Subject: Concern about precise location collection in release 5.2

Hi Sam, before we ship 5.2 I want to flag a risk. The change collects users' exact GPS location
without a consent prompt or privacy notice update.

Why it matters: GDPR requires consent for this data; app store policies require disclosure;
rejection or fines could delay the release far more than a legal review would.

Proposal: ship 5.2 with coarse region data behind the existing consent prompt, and add precise
location in 5.3 after the privacy team's review (I've asked them for a slot this week).

I'm not comfortable implementing collection without consent, but I'm happy to help find the
fastest compliant path. Copying Lena (privacy) for visibility.
```

## 🎯 Interview Cheat Sheet

**30-second pitch:** For failure questions I pick a real setback, own it without blame, explain what I learned and what I changed — in process, design or behavior — and show later evidence that it worked. For burnout I make load visible with data, reduce toil, protect focus and ask for help early; as a lead I treat it as a system problem. For ethical dilemmas I raise concerns clearly in business terms through the right channels, offer alternatives, and refuse to do anything illegal or harmful.

**Must know:**
* Failure story: real stakes, ownership, learning, change, evidence.
* Burnout signs and sustainable practices; lead responsibilities.
* Ethical escalation: facts, channels, alternatives, written record.
* A STAR story for each with outcomes.

**Common follow-ups:**
* What did your manager say about the failure?
* Have you ever pushed back on leadership?
* How do you prevent burnout in an on-call rotation?

**Red flags:** fake failures; blaming others; "I'd just do what I'm told"; glorifying overtime.
