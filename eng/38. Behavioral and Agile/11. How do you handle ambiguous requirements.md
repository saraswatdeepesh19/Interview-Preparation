# How do you handle ambiguous requirements

---

## 🟢 Junior Level

Ambiguity is normal: stakeholders describe goals ("customers should be able to export their data"), not specifications. The interviewer wants to see that you **reduce ambiguity actively** instead of guessing silently or waiting passively.

### A simple approach
1. **Restate the goal** in your own words and confirm it: *who* needs *what* and *why*.
2. **Ask clarifying questions** — scope, users, volume, edge cases, deadlines, what "done" looks like.
3. **Write down assumptions** and share them ("I'm assuming exports are CSV and under 100k rows — correct?").
4. **Propose a small first version** and get feedback early.
5. **Confirm acceptance criteria** before investing heavily.

### Questions that uncover hidden requirements
* Who will use this, and how often?
* What happens today without this feature?
* What's the expected data volume (rows, file size, requests)?
* Any security/privacy constraints (PII, permissions, GDPR)?
* How will we know it works? What does success look like (metric)?
* What's explicitly **out of scope**?

## 🟡 Middle Level

### Turning ambiguity into concrete work
* **Example mapping** with product and QA: rules → concrete examples → open questions. Questions you can't answer become explicit follow-ups with owners.
* **Acceptance criteria in Given/When/Then** form for the main flows and edge cases.
* **Spikes** (time-boxed investigations) for technical unknowns: "Can our reporting DB export 1M rows in under a minute?"
* **Walking skeleton / thin vertical slice**: the simplest end-to-end version (UI button → API → async job → file in S3 → download link) to validate the shape before polishing.

### STAR example

**Situation**: Product asked for "data export for enterprise customers" with a two-sentence ticket; customers had up to 5M orders.

**Task**: Deliver a usable export without overloading the production database.

**Action**:
* Interviewed two account managers and one customer: they needed monthly order exports for accounting, filtered by date, in CSV, and accepted results by email/download within an hour.
* Documented assumptions and non-goals (no real-time export, no custom columns in v1) and got product sign-off.
* Ran a one-day spike: a naive `SELECT` streamed over HTTP timed out at ~1M rows and caused replica lag.
* Designed an async export: `POST /exports` → `202 Accepted` with an operation resource, a worker using keyset pagination on a read replica, gzip CSV to S3, presigned download link, email notification. Rate-limited to two concurrent exports per tenant.

**Result**: Shipped v1 in three weeks; largest export (4.8M rows) completed in 6 minutes with no impact on production latency. The explicit non-goals avoided scope creep; custom columns came in v2 based on actual usage data.

## 🔴 Senior Level

### What strong answers demonstrate
* **Ownership of clarity**: you don't wait for perfect specs; you drive the conversation and write things down (one-page brief, RFC, or ticket updates).
* **Judgment about which ambiguity matters**: some decisions are reversible (column order) — decide and move on; others are expensive (data model, public API, security model) — clarify before building.
* **Stakeholder alignment**: you involve the right people (product, design, security, operations, support) early, and make trade-offs explicit ("real-time export doubles the effort and risks DB load — is it worth it?").
* **Iterative delivery**: you deliver value incrementally and use feedback/metrics to resolve remaining ambiguity.
* **Communication of uncertainty**: estimates as ranges with assumptions; flagging risks early rather than late.

### Pitfalls to avoid in your answer
* "I built exactly what the ticket said" (no thinking about users or edge cases).
* "I waited for the product owner to clarify" (passivity).
* "I built the most flexible solution possible" (over-engineering to cover every interpretation).
* Not mentioning how you confirmed the result met the need.

### Adapting the story to seniority
* Junior/mid: clarifying questions, writing acceptance criteria, spikes, early demos.
* Senior/lead: reframing the problem with the business, defining success metrics, aligning multiple teams, making scope trade-offs, mentoring others to handle ambiguity.

### Assumptions note you can send after the first conversation

```markdown
Subject: Data export v1 — assumptions for confirmation

Goal: enterprise customers export monthly orders for accounting.
Assumptions:
1. CSV only, fixed columns (id, date, status, net, tax, gross, currency).
2. Up to 5M rows per export; delivery within 1 hour is acceptable.
3. Only users with the ADMIN role of the tenant can export.
Out of scope for v1: custom columns, scheduled exports, Excel format.
Open questions: Do exports include cancelled orders? (owner: PM, by Thursday)
```

## 🎯 Interview Cheat Sheet

**30-second pitch:** I reduce ambiguity actively: restate the goal, ask about users, volume, constraints and success criteria, write down assumptions and non-goals for sign-off, use example mapping and spikes for unknowns, and deliver a thin vertical slice early to get feedback. I separate reversible decisions, which I make quickly, from expensive ones like data models or public APIs, which I clarify first.

**Must know:**
* Clarifying question checklist.
* Assumptions and non-goals in writing.
* Example mapping, acceptance criteria, spikes, walking skeleton.
* Reversible vs irreversible decisions.
* A concrete STAR story with measurable results.

**Common follow-ups:**
* What if stakeholders disagree on the requirement?
* What if you discover the requirement was wrong after building it?
* How do you estimate ambiguous work?

**Red flags:** passivity; building to the letter without questions; over-engineering for every interpretation.
