# Retrospectives and continuous improvement

---

## 🟢 Junior Level

A **retrospective** is a regular meeting (in Scrum, at the end of each sprint) where the team reflects on **how it works** — process, tools, collaboration — and agrees on improvements. It's the engine of continuous improvement (*kaizen*).

### A simple format (60 minutes for a two-week sprint)
1. **Set the stage** (5 min) — goal, safety check ("how comfortable are you to speak openly, 1–5?").
2. **Gather data** (15 min) — what went well, what didn't, what puzzled us; timeline of the sprint; metrics.
3. **Generate insights** (15 min) — group themes, find causes ("5 whys", fishbone).
4. **Decide what to do** (15 min) — pick **1–3 concrete actions** with owners.
5. **Close** (5 min) — appreciation, quick feedback on the retro itself.

### What makes retros useful
* **Safety** — people can speak honestly without blame (the Prime Directive: "everyone did the best job they could, given what they knew at the time").
* **Few, concrete actions** — "Add a PR size check to CI by Friday (owner: Ana)", not "communicate better".
* **Follow-through** — start each retro by reviewing last time's actions.

## 🟡 Middle Level

### Bring data, not just feelings
Feelings matter, but data sharpens discussion:
* **Delivery metrics**: cycle time, PR review time, deployment frequency, change failure rate.
* **Quality signals**: escaped defects, flaky test count, incidents and pages per on-call week.
* **Flow**: work in progress, unplanned work percentage, blocked items and why.

Example: "Average PR waited 26 hours for first review; 4 of 9 stories missed the sprint because of review delays" leads to a concrete experiment (review SLA, pairing, smaller PRs) better than "reviews are slow".

### Formats to avoid monotony
* **Start / Stop / Continue**
* **Mad / Sad / Glad**
* **Sailboat** (wind = helps, anchors = slows, rocks = risks, island = goal)
* **4Ls** (Liked, Learned, Lacked, Longed for)
* **Timeline** retro for eventful sprints or after incidents/releases
* **Lean Coffee** — participants propose topics, vote, timebox discussions

### Treat actions as experiments
Frame improvements as hypotheses with a check date: "If we cap WIP at 3 per person, cycle time drops below 4 days — review in two sprints." Keep what works, drop what doesn't.

## 🔴 Senior Level

### STAR example

**Situation**: Our team's retros had become a ritual: the same complaints ("too many meetings", "flaky tests") and actions nobody followed up on.

**Task**: As tech lead, make retros produce real improvement.

**Action**:
* Rotated facilitation among team members and varied formats.
* Added a 5-minute data review at the start (cycle time trend, flaky test leaderboard from CI, on-call pages).
* Limited outcomes to two actions per retro, each with an owner and a ticket on the sprint board; reviewed them first thing next retro.
* Escalated systemic issues outside the team's control (e.g. slow shared staging environment) with data to the platform lead.

**Result**: In three months, the flaky-test count dropped from 23 to 3 (one action per retro targeted the top offenders), median cycle time fell from 6 to 3.5 days after we introduced a review rotation, and retro attendance and engagement increased noticeably (psych-safety check average from 2.8 to 4.1).

### Beyond the sprint retro
* **Incident postmortems** and **release retros** are retrospectives with a specific focus.
* **Cross-team retros** for shared problems (dependencies, platform pain points).
* **Continuous improvement outside meetings**: fix small annoyances immediately (boy scout rule), keep an improvement backlog, allocate capacity (e.g. 10–20%) for engineering effectiveness.
* Measure improvement over time with DORA and flow metrics, and developer satisfaction surveys.

### Anti-patterns
* Blame sessions, or management using retro content against people.
* Too many actions → none done.
* Only discussing things outside the team's control (venting) without escalation paths.
* Skipping retros when "busy" — exactly when they're most needed.

### Turning a retro discussion into an experiment

```markdown
Observation (data): median PR wait for first review = 26 h; 4/9 stories spilled over because of review delays.
Hypothesis: if every PR gets a first review within 4 working hours, cycle time drops below 4 days.
Experiment: daily review rotation (one "reviewer on duty"), PRs < 400 lines, Slack reminder after 3 h.
Owner: Jonas · Check: retro on 2026-11-13 using the CI review-latency dashboard.
Keep if: median first review < 4 h and cycle time < 4 days. Otherwise: discuss alternatives.
```

## 🎯 Interview Cheat Sheet

**30-second pitch:** Retrospectives are regular, safe reflections on how the team works that end with one to three concrete, owned actions reviewed next time. I bring data — cycle time, review latency, flaky tests, incidents — vary formats, frame actions as experiments with check dates, escalate systemic issues with evidence, and extend the practice to postmortems, release and cross-team retros, measuring progress with flow and DORA metrics.

**Must know:**
* Five-phase retro structure; Prime Directive; psychological safety.
* Data-informed retros.
* Formats: Start/Stop/Continue, Sailboat, 4Ls, timeline, Lean Coffee.
* Actions as experiments; follow-through.
* Anti-patterns.

**Common follow-ups:**
* What improvement from a retro are you proud of?
* How do you handle a retro where nobody speaks?
* What if the problem is outside the team's control?

**Red flags:** retros without actions; blame; "we stopped doing retros because nothing changed".
