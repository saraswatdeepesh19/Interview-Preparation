# Estimation and missed deadlines

---

## 🟢 Junior Level

Interviewers ask about estimation and missed deadlines to see whether you **communicate uncertainty honestly** and **raise risks early**. Everyone misses estimates sometimes; what matters is how you handle it.

### Estimation basics
* **Break work down** into small tasks (≤ 1–2 days each); unknown tasks become spikes.
* **Estimate ranges**, not single numbers: "3–5 days, most likely 4".
* State **assumptions**: "assuming the payment provider's sandbox is available and the API matches the docs".
* Include the **forgotten work**: tests, code review, migrations, deployment, documentation, monitoring, edge cases, bug fixing.
* Compare with **past similar work** (reference class), not with how long it "should" take.

### When you're going to miss a deadline
1. **Tell people as soon as you know** — not on the due date.
2. Explain **why** (new information, scope change, unexpected complexity) and the **new forecast**.
3. Offer **options**: reduce scope, add help, change the date, accept a phased delivery.
4. **Learn**: what signal did you miss earlier?

## 🟡 Middle Level

### Agile estimation techniques
* **Story points** with planning poker — relative sizing; useful for team forecasting via velocity, not for converting to hours or comparing teams.
* **T-shirt sizes** for roadmap-level sizing.
* **No-estimates / flow metrics** — slice work small and forecast with throughput and cycle time distributions (e.g. Monte Carlo simulation: "85% chance of finishing these 30 items in 6–8 weeks").
* **Three-point estimates** (optimistic, most likely, pessimistic) → PERT expected value `(O + 4M + P) / 6` with explicit uncertainty.

### Why estimates go wrong
* Unknown unknowns (integration surprises, legacy behavior, data quality).
* Optimism bias and planning fallacy; estimating only the happy path.
* Hidden dependencies on other teams or external vendors.
* Interruptions (on-call, support, meetings) not accounted for.
* Scope creep without re-estimation.

### STAR example

**Situation**: I estimated two weeks for migrating our notification service from a self-hosted SMTP relay to AWS SES, committed for a release date tied to a marketing campaign.

**Task**: Deliver the migration in time for the campaign.

**Action**: On day four I discovered that bounce and complaint handling required SNS webhooks and suppression list logic we didn't have — about a week of extra work. The same day I informed my lead and the product owner with three options: (1) slip the full migration by one week, (2) migrate transactional emails now and keep marketing emails on the old relay for the campaign, (3) launch without bounce handling (I recommended against it due to sender reputation risk). We chose option 2. I split the work, delivered transactional emails on time, and completed bounce handling the following week.

**Result**: The campaign ran on schedule; the full migration finished one week later than originally planned with no deliverability issues. In the retro I added "external provider integration checklist" (webhooks, quotas, sandbox limits) to our estimation template, and our next provider integration was estimated within 10%.

## 🔴 Senior Level

### Leading estimation for a team or project
* **Decompose to reduce variance**: vertical slices that can be delivered independently; identify the riskiest parts and **front-load** them (spikes, prototypes).
* **Forecast with data**: throughput and cycle time from the team's history; communicate probabilistic forecasts ("80% confidence by March 15, 95% by March 29").
* **Manage scope as the main lever**: fix the date, flex scope (MVP vs nice-to-haves, MoSCoW prioritization) — or fix scope, flex date. Adding people late rarely helps (Brooks's law).
* **Track early indicators**: burn-up charts with scope line, unplanned work ratio, dependency status; re-forecast weekly.
* **Make dependencies explicit**: other teams' deliverables, vendor contracts, security reviews — with dates and owners.

### Handling pressure to commit to unrealistic dates
* Separate **estimate** (what we think it takes) from **target** (what the business wants) and **commitment** (what we agree to after trade-offs).
* Present trade-offs transparently: "To hit June 1, we can deliver A and B; C would need until June 20 or an extra engineer from team X for three weeks."
* Never pad silently or agree to impossible dates to avoid conflict — it destroys trust later.

### Forecasting with your own history (Monte Carlo)

```java
// Weekly throughput (items done) of the last 12 weeks -> how many weeks for 30 remaining items?
int[] history = {4, 6, 3, 5, 7, 2, 5, 6, 4, 5, 3, 6};
int remaining = 30, runs = 10_000;
int[] weeksNeeded = new int[runs];
var rnd = new java.util.Random(42);
for (int r = 0; r < runs; r++) {
    int done = 0, weeks = 0;
    while (done < remaining) { done += history[rnd.nextInt(history.length)]; weeks++; }
    weeksNeeded[r] = weeks;
}
java.util.Arrays.sort(weeksNeeded);
System.out.printf("50%%: %d weeks, 85%%: %d weeks, 95%%: %d weeks%n",
        weeksNeeded[runs / 2], weeksNeeded[(int) (runs * 0.85)], weeksNeeded[(int) (runs * 0.95)]);
```

Communicating "85% likely within 8 weeks" (what this history produces) with the data behind it is far more credible than a single date.

## 🎯 Interview Cheat Sheet

**30-second pitch:** I estimate by breaking work into small pieces, giving ranges with explicit assumptions, including testing, review, migration and deployment work, and comparing with past work; for teams I forecast with throughput data and front-load risky parts. When a deadline is at risk I communicate as soon as I know, explain why, and offer options — reduce scope, phase delivery, add help or move the date — and then learn from what I missed.

**Must know:**
* Ranges + assumptions; forgotten work; spikes.
* Story points, three-point estimates, flow-based forecasting.
* Causes of estimate errors.
* Early communication with options.
* Estimate vs target vs commitment; scope as the lever.

**Common follow-ups:**
* How do you estimate something you've never done?
* What do you do when management insists on a date?
* Story points or hours?

**Red flags:** hiding delays until the deadline; blaming others; single-number estimates with no assumptions.
