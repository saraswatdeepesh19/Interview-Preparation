# Mentoring juniors and leading without authority

---

## 🟢 Junior Level

Senior engineers are expected to **multiply** the team's impact, not just their own output. Two common interview topics:
* **Mentoring** — helping less experienced engineers grow skills, confidence and judgment.
* **Leading without authority** — influencing technical direction, standards and other teams without being their manager.

### Mentoring basics
* **Listen first**: understand their goals, current skills, and how they like to learn.
* **Teach reasoning**: explain *why*, not just *what*; ask guiding questions instead of giving answers immediately.
* **Give safe stretch tasks**: slightly beyond current ability, with support available.
* **Give regular, specific feedback**: what to keep doing, what to improve, with examples.
* **Celebrate progress** publicly; correct privately.

### Leading without authority basics
* Earn **credibility** through consistently good work and helpfulness.
* **Explain the problem** and benefits before proposing solutions.
* **Make it easy** for others to follow (templates, tools, examples).
* **Build allies** — involve people early so ideas become shared.

## 🟡 Middle Level

### A mentoring toolkit
* **Pair programming** — let the mentee drive; you navigate, ask "what would happen if…?".
* **Code review as teaching** — label comments (blocking/suggestion/nit), link to principles or docs, praise good decisions.
* **Graduated ownership**: bug fixes → small features → a component → leading a small project → presenting a design (RFC) to the team.
* **On-call shadowing** — join incidents as an observer, then as a secondary, then primary with backup.
* **1:1s with growth goals** — e.g. "able to design and ship a REST endpoint with tests and observability independently within 3 months".
* **Reading lists and katas** — targeted resources (e.g. *Effective Java* chapters on equals/hashCode) linked to real tasks.

### Influencing without authority
* **Write a short RFC** describing a problem with data, options and a recommendation; invite feedback broadly.
* **Prototype** to make the idea concrete (a working ArchUnit rule set, a pipeline template, a benchmark).
* **Start small** with one team or service, show measurable results, then expand.
* **Understand incentives**: what does each team or stakeholder gain or lose? Address their concerns explicitly.
* **Use forums**: architecture guilds, chapter meetings, internal talks, documentation.

## 🔴 Senior Level

### STAR example: mentoring

**Situation**: Two junior developers joined our payments team; both were strong in Java syntax but had never worked with transactions, idempotency or production operations.

**Task**: Get them productive and confident on a high-risk domain within one quarter.

**Action**:
* Set individual goals in the first 1:1 and a 12-week plan: weeks 1–3 pairing on small changes; weeks 4–8 owning features with design check-ins; weeks 9–12 leading a small project.
* Ran weekly 45-minute "deep dive" sessions on concepts we used daily (isolation levels, outbox, idempotency keys), each tied to our code.
* Reviewed their PRs with explanations and links, and asked them to review mine.
* Had them shadow on-call for two weeks before joining the rotation.

**Result**: By the end of the quarter, one led the implementation of idempotent refund processing (designed via an RFC she presented), the other joined on-call and handled two incidents with only light support. Both later mentored the next new hires.

### STAR example: leading without authority

**Situation**: Five teams each had their own CI pipeline configuration; builds averaged 35 minutes and security scanning was inconsistent.

**Task**: Standardize pipelines without managerial authority over the other teams.

**Action**: Collected build metrics across teams, wrote an RFC with a reusable GitHub Actions workflow (caching, parallel tests, dependency and image scanning), piloted it on my team (build time down to 11 minutes), presented results at the engineering guild, offered pairing sessions to migrate each team, and incorporated their feedback (opt-in steps for monorepos, custom test commands).

**Result**: Four of five teams adopted it within two months; median build time across teams dropped by ~60%, and all services gained consistent vulnerability scanning. The fifth team adopted it later after we added a feature they needed.

### Leadership qualities interviewers look for
* **Empathy and patience**, adapting style to the person.
* **Clarity** — written communication that scales influence.
* **Generosity with credit** and visible support for others' ideas.
* **Persistence without pushing** — iterating on proposals based on feedback.
* **Knowing when to escalate** — involving managers for priority conflicts rather than forcing decisions.

### A simple 12-week growth plan for a new team member

```markdown
Goal: independently design, ship and operate a payment-related feature by week 12.

Weeks 1-3  Pair on 3 small changes; read ADRs 1-15; local setup + first deploy on day 2
Weeks 4-8  Own 2 features with design check-ins; review 2 PRs per week; deep dives: isolation levels, outbox, idempotency
Weeks 9-10 Shadow on-call; write one runbook improvement
Weeks 11-12 Lead a small RFC (e.g. refund retries) and present it to the team

Check-ins: weekly 1:1 (30 min) — what went well, where stuck, next stretch task
Signals of progress: PR review rounds trend down, asks better questions, explains trade-offs unprompted
```

## 🎯 Interview Cheat Sheet

**30-second pitch:** I mentor by understanding goals, teaching reasoning through pairing and reviews, giving graduated ownership and specific feedback, and creating safe stretch opportunities like RFCs and on-call shadowing. I lead without authority through credibility, data-backed proposals, prototypes and pilots that show measurable results, addressing stakeholders' incentives and making adoption easy — with STAR examples that show outcomes.

**Must know:**
* Mentoring toolkit: pairing, review as teaching, graduated ownership, 1:1 goals, shadowing.
* Influence toolkit: RFCs, prototypes, pilots, guilds, incentives.
* Two concrete STAR stories with metrics.
* Leadership qualities: empathy, clarity, credit, persistence, escalation judgment.

**Common follow-ups:**
* How do you handle a mentee who isn't improving?
* What if another team refuses your proposal?
* How do you balance mentoring with your own deliverables?

**Red flags:** "I just tell them what to do"; taking credit for others' work; forcing standards through mandates only.
