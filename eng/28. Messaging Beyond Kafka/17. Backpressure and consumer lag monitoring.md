# Backpressure and consumer lag monitoring

---

## 🟢 Junior Level

**Backpressure** is how a system copes when producers send messages faster than consumers can process them. In messaging, the broker acts as a buffer: messages accumulate in the queue or log. The amount of unprocessed work is the **consumer lag** (Kafka) or **queue depth** (RabbitMQ, SQS).

Some lag is normal during bursts. Lag that **keeps growing** means consumers are too slow, and eventually:
* messages get old — business latency rises (an order email arrives an hour late),
* retention deletes unread messages (Kafka) or queues hit limits (RabbitMQ memory alarms, `x-max-length`),
* recovery takes long because consumers must chew through a huge backlog.

### What to measure
* **Kafka**: lag per partition = log-end offset − committed offset; better, **time lag** (age of the oldest unconsumed message).
* **RabbitMQ**: `messages_ready`, `messages_unacknowledged`, publish vs deliver/ack rates.
* **SQS**: `ApproximateNumberOfMessagesVisible`, `ApproximateAgeOfOldestMessage`.

---

## 🟡 Middle Level

### Consumer-side flow control
* **RabbitMQ prefetch** (`basic.qos`) — how many unacked messages a consumer may hold. Low prefetch spreads load fairly and protects slow consumers; too low limits throughput on high-latency links.

```yaml
spring:
  rabbitmq:
    listener:
      simple:
        prefetch: 20
        concurrency: 4
        max-concurrency: 16       # scales consumers between 4 and 16 based on load
```

* **Kafka pull model** — consumers fetch at their own pace (`max.poll.records`, `fetch.max.bytes`); slow processing must still call `poll()` within `max.poll.interval.ms` (default 5 min) or the consumer is kicked out of the group, triggering a rebalance (and duplicate processing).

```yaml
spring:
  kafka:
    consumer:
      max-poll-records: 200
      properties:
        max.poll.interval.ms: 300000
    listener:
      concurrency: 6              # ≤ number of partitions
```

### Scaling consumers
* RabbitMQ/SQS: add consumers or instances freely.
* Kafka: parallelism is capped by partitions; more partitions require a topic change (and affect key-to-partition mapping for future messages). For CPU-heavy processing, consider **parallel processing within a partition** while preserving per-key order (Confluent Parallel Consumer).
* Autoscale on lag: **KEDA** scalers for Kafka lag, RabbitMQ queue length, SQS depth drive Kubernetes replicas.

### Producer-side backpressure
* RabbitMQ applies **flow control** (blocks publishers' connections) under memory or disk alarms — your HTTP threads publishing synchronously will block too.
* Kafka producers block on `buffer.memory` exhaustion for up to `max.block.ms` (default 60 s), then throw. Set it low and handle failures (outbox helps decouple).

## 🔴 Senior Level

### Alerting on the right signal
Offset lag alone misleads: 10,000 messages behind on a topic with 50,000 msg/s is 0.2 s; on a topic with 1 msg/min it's a week. Alert on:
* **Time lag / oldest message age** vs the business SLO (e.g. "order events processed within 60 s").
* **Lag trend** — growing for N minutes while producers are steady.
* **Consumer group health** — no active members, frequent rebalances, stuck partitions (lag growing on one partition only → poison message or hot key).

Tools: Burrow or Kafka Lag Exporter (time lag estimation), Kafka's `kafka-consumer-groups.sh --describe`, Micrometer `kafka.consumer.fetch.manager.records.lag.max`, RabbitMQ Prometheus plugin, CloudWatch for SQS.

### Root causes of lag and fixes
* **Slow downstream** (DB, external API) — batch writes, increase concurrency up to the downstream's capacity, cache lookups; bulkhead the downstream rather than unbounded concurrency.
* **Poison message** — repeated failures block a partition (Kafka) or cycle through requeues (RabbitMQ). Bounded retries + DLQ/DLT (`DefaultErrorHandler` with `DeadLetterPublishingRecoverer`, `@RetryableTopic` non-blocking retries).
* **Hot partition / key** — one key carries most traffic. Re-key, sub-partition (key + bucket) if ordering allows, or isolate the hot tenant's topic.
* **Rebalance storms** — long processing exceeding `max.poll.interval.ms`, aggressive autoscaling. Use cooperative-sticky assignment, static membership (`group.instance.id`), and keep batch processing time bounded.
* **GC or CPU starvation** in consumers — profile; tune batch sizes; separate CPU-heavy work.

### Shedding and prioritization
When lag is unavoidable (an outage upstream), decide what can be dropped or deferred: skip stale messages past their usefulness (`if (event.age() > Duration.ofMinutes(30)) skip`), process high-priority queues first (RabbitMQ priority queues or separate topics), or compact to latest state (Kafka compacted topics for state updates).

### Capacity planning
Measure **max sustainable throughput per consumer instance**; required instances = peak produce rate / per-instance throughput × safety factor. For Kafka, partitions ≥ max needed consumers (with headroom for growth), and **catch-up rate** after an outage: backlog / (consume rate − produce rate) = recovery time — make sure it meets your SLO.

## 🎯 Interview Cheat Sheet

**30-second pitch:** Brokers absorb bursts, so backpressure shows up as consumer lag or queue depth. I control flow with prefetch (RabbitMQ) and poll settings (Kafka), scale consumers — bounded by partitions in Kafka — possibly autoscaling on lag with KEDA, and alert on time lag against business SLOs rather than raw offsets. Typical causes are slow downstreams, poison messages, hot partitions and rebalance storms.

**Must know:**
* Lag = log-end − committed offset; time lag is more meaningful.
* Prefetch, `max.poll.records`, `max.poll.interval.ms`.
* Partitions cap Kafka consumer parallelism.
* DLQ/DLT and bounded retries for poison messages.
* Catch-up time = backlog / (consume − produce rate).

**Common follow-ups:**
* Lag grows on one partition only — why?
* How do you autoscale consumers?
* What happens when RabbitMQ hits its memory alarm?

**Red flags:** alerting on fixed offset thresholds; unlimited retries in place; scaling Kafka consumers beyond partition count.
